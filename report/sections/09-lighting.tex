\subsection{Illumination and De-lighting}

Scene illumination is estimated from the panorama with
LuxDiT~\cite{liang2025luxdit}, which recovers an HDR environment and a dominant
light direction. The client uses both: the environment as an image-based lighting
source, and the direction as a directional light casting the scene's shadows.

  \begin{subfigure}{0.46\linewidth}

    \caption{Log scale.}
  \end{subfigure}
  \caption{Estimated HDR environment for the Rainier capture. The tone-mapped view
  shows what the eye would see; the log view exposes the dynamic range the client
  actually samples, with the sun as a compact high-intensity lobe well above the
  surrounding sky. The dominant direction extracted from this lobe drives the
  scene's directional light.}
  \label{fig:rainier-lighting}
\end{figure}

Every asset in the scene is cut from a photograph, so it already carries the
illumination of the moment it was taken: shadows, directional shading, and the
color of the light. Rendering that under a second, freshly estimated light doubles
the illumination and the result reads as flat and over-exposed. Object crops are
therefore de-lit with IntrinsicDiffusion~\cite{Luo2024IntrinsicDiffusion}, which
separates albedo from shading, so what the client relights is material color rather
than a photograph.

De-lighting is what makes the objects portable. Population synthesis places
instances anywhere the class is admissible, not only where they were photographed,
so an instance rarely sits in the orientation its shading was baked under. An
asset carrying its original shading would announce this immediately, lit from one
direction while the terrain beneath it is lit from another.

\subsection{Motion}

A short video is generated from the panorama with
LTX-2~\cite{lightricks2026ltx2}, and each detected object is tracked through it
with SAM~2~\cite{ravi2024sam2segmentimages}. The camera is held locked off during
generation, which is what makes the resulting tracks usable: any drift in an
object's image-space centroid is the object moving, not the camera.

The classification itself is a single measurement. An object whose centroid drifts
less than \SI{60}{\percent} of its own bounding-box diagonal over the whole clip is
treated as \emph{stationary}: it is oscillating in place, anchored to the scene, as
a flower or a tree is. Anything that drifts further is a \emph{moving} body.

Stationary objects contribute an amplitude and a frequency, read from their own
horizontal centroid signal, and are rigged rather than animated directly. Each
shared bucket mesh gets a three-bone vertical skeleton baked into it, with joints
at the base of the mesh and at one third and two thirds of its height. The last
joint sits at two thirds rather than at the top for a specific reason: a joint
bends what is above it, so a joint at the very top has no geometry to move and
degenerates into a dead bone, hinging the whole upper half about a single point
instead of curving along its length. With joints at thirds, the bottom third stays
rigid on the ground and each joint above inherits the bend below it plus its own.
Timing is attached per instance while the skeleton stays part of the shared mesh,
so a meadow of thousands of plants remains a single instanced draw with the sway
evaluated per instance.

Moving objects are handled as rigid bodies. Their image-space track is unprojected
into world space, holding depth fixed at the reference frame's estimate since no
per-frame depth exists, and fitted with a smoothed curve to recover a linear
velocity and acceleration. Those become the initial conditions handed to the
client's physics engine, which then resolves the object against the terrain and
against other objects. Angular velocity is deliberately out of scope: it is not
reliably recoverable from a monocular silhouette, and a wrong spin is more
noticeable than none.

\subsection{A Shared Wind Field}
\label{sec:wind}

Foliage sway, grass motion and the water surface are driven by one scene-global
wind field rather than by independent per-object oscillators. The field is an
ambient base strength plus discrete gust events, and it is what gives the scene its
sense of connectedness: a gust crosses the meadow and everything in its path
responds in turn, rather than each plant oscillating on its own clock. Independent
oscillators are cheaper and read as obviously synthetic, because nothing in a real
landscape moves without its neighbors moving too.

The field is currently authored rather than derived. The per-object amplitudes and
frequencies recovered above set how strongly each instance responds to it, but the
gust schedule itself does not yet come from the generated video. Deriving it from
the observed correlated motion, so that the reconstructed wind matches the wind in
the photograph, is left as future work.
