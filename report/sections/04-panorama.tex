\section{The Panoramic Substrate}
\label{sec:panorama}
% =============================================================================

Every track in \cref{fig:pipeline} consumes the same three artefacts: a
$360^\circ$ panorama, a metric depth map registered to it, and a per-pixel
semantic typing of it. This section covers how those are produced and the two
properties of them that constrain everything downstream --- the depth map's
metric compression, and the fact that the panorama must be maintained in more
than one version.

\subsection{Panorama generation and metric calibration}

The input image is captioned, its perspective depth estimated, and the image
outpainted to a full equirectangular panorama, then super-resolved. Panoramic
depth is estimated on the result~\cite{lin2025dap}.

That depth is relative, so it is calibrated against the one metric anchor
available: an assumed camera height above the ground directly below. Two
properties of the calibration matter downstream. First, depth beyond the
hero-photo overlap is a capped extrapolation. Second, a far-range compression
folds everything beyond a fraction of the maximum range into the maximum, because
the underlying prediction saturates there. The result is \emph{angularly faithful
but metrically compressed}: the panorama lines up, but distances --- and anything
derived by multiplying by distance --- are systematically shrunk.

We flag this explicitly because it is the origin of a defect that appears far
downstream. Object size is computed as angular extent times depth, so objects
inherit the compression and render too small. Correcting the depth map would
change the terrain, which is derived from the same map and is otherwise correct,
so instead the correction is applied at placement time to object extents only,
using per-class real-world height priors pooled into a monotonic depth-to-factor
curve. Positions, terrain and both depth maps are untouched.

\subsection{Semantic region map}

In parallel, a semantic region map is produced by projecting per-pixel region
types (sky, water, terrain, ground, vegetation, built, road, trail) from the
panorama onto the same grid. This map drives water extraction, texture material
selection, ground-cover masking and, as \cref{sec:results} discusses, several
plausibility checks on object detections.

Two typings are maintained, one from the original panorama and one from the
object-removed panorama. This is not redundancy. Foreground inpainting removes
everything closer than a threshold, which in a ground-level capture is the ground
itself across the whole lower hemisphere. On one alpine capture the removal mask
covered \SI{48.5}{\percent} of the panorama and \SI{100}{\percent} of every row
below roughly $60\%$ height, and the meadow it erased returned as bare gravel.
Sampled over directly measured cells, the vegetation evidence fell from
\SI{1000}{\meter\squared} to \SI{47}{\meter\squared}. Geometry must come from the
inpainted panorama; semantics must come from the original.
